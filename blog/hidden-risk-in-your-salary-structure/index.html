The Hidden Risk in Your Salary Structure

Splitting pay into a low basic and a large special allowance can leave PF unpaid. See a worked example and five red flags.

Payroll22 Oct 20265 min readBy TeamIndia Compliance Team

Splitting pay into a small basic and a large "special allowance" can look like a harmless payroll choice. If that allowance is really regular wages, the structure can leave PF unpaid, and the exposure grows every month it continues.

The salary-splitting trap: an illustration

This example is hypothetical and simplified, to show the mechanics. It assumes the statutory PF wage ceiling of ₹25,000 a month (raised from ₹15,000 on 17 September 2026; see our wage ceiling post) and contributions at 12% each from employer and employee. Check the current ceiling before using these numbers.

A company pays an employee ₹40,000 a month. It fixes basic pay at ₹10,000 and calls the other ₹30,000 "special allowance", paid to every employee every month.

Allowance treated as outside wagesAllowance treated as wages
PF wage considered₹10,000₹25,000 (ceiling)
Employer PF at 12%₹1,200₹3,000
Employee PF at 12%₹1,200₹3,000
Combined monthly gap₹3,600

That is ₹43,200 a year for one employee. For a 100-person team it is ₹43.2 lakh a year before interest and any damages, and it compounds across the years an authority can look back over. Where employees earn less than the ceiling, the whole allowance can matter, not just part of it.

Why the name on the payslip does not decide it

In Vivekananda Vidyamandir the Supreme Court looked past the label. An allowance paid universally, necessarily and ordinarily to all across the board is basic wages. Only payments that depend on something an employee has to earn, such as extra work or a performance target, fall outside.

The Court also noted that the employers had produced nothing to show their allowances were variable or incentive-linked. Documentation is part of the defence.

Red flags in a salary structure

  1. One large "special allowance" that is the same for every employee in a grade.
  2. A basic pay set at a round, low figure with the rest spread across allowances nobody can explain.
  3. Allowances with no conditions: no eligibility rule, no link to attendance, output or extra duty.
  4. Structures that changed around the time PF coverage began, with no business reason on file.
  5. No written rationale for why each component sits where it does.

How to fix it

  • Rebuild the pay structure with a stated purpose for every component.
  • Move genuinely variable pay into clearly conditional, documented schemes.
  • Do not cut employees' take-home pay to absorb the change without legal advice; employment terms and contract law still apply.
  • Reconcile past PF payments and decide, with advice, how to correct any gap.

General information, not legal advice. Source for the legal test: Supreme Court judgment (Indian Kanoon).
